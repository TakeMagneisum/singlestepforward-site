Accessibility & inclusion

Understanding reduces friction. Respect increases access.

A disability may affect how someone reads, communicates, learns, moves, senses, processes information, or participates. The useful response is not assumption—it is asking what access would help.

Learn by topic ↓ Everyday inclusion

A practical starting point

Make the next interaction easier.

Access needs vary. These habits are useful because they make room for the person to tell you what works instead of making you guess.

  1. 01

    Ask, do not assume.

    Try: “What would make this easier to use or understand?” Respect the answer and the person’s right to decline help.

  2. 02

    Speak to the person.

    Address the person directly, even when an interpreter, aide, family member, or support person is present.

  3. 03

    Offer more than one format.

    Plain language, written steps, captions, alt text, larger text, audio, and extra processing time can remove different barriers.

  4. 04

    Assume competence.

    Communication style, speech, movement, reading speed, or support needs do not reveal everything a person understands or can decide.

  5. 05

    Treat access as access.

    An accommodation is a way to participate—not an unfair advantage, a favor, or a reason for pity.

Access & rights

Accessibility basics

Accessibility removes barriers in information, technology, communication, buildings, services, and participation. It is essential for some people and useful for many more.

Reading, writing & processing

Learning disabilities

Learning disabilities can affect reading, writing, math, language, organization, or information processing. They do not tell you a person’s effort, character, or overall potential.

03

Language & participation

Down syndrome

Down syndrome is a genetic condition, not a disease. In the United States, “person with Down syndrome” is widely preferred; avoid “suffers from,” “afflicted by,” and “Down’s.” Follow the individual’s own language preference.

NDSS preferred-language guide ↗ NDSS resources across the lifespan ↗
04

Neurodiversity & self-advocacy

Autism and neurodiversity

Use the language a person prefers. Do not infer intelligence, independence, or support needs from speech, eye contact, movement, or appearance. Communication support is access.

Choice, rights & community

Intellectual and developmental disabilities

People with intellectual and developmental disabilities have different strengths and support needs. Inclusion means access to choice, communication, community life, education, work, relationships, and self-advocacy.

U.S. workplace access

Workplace accommodations

A workplace accommodation can change how, where, or when work is done so a qualified person can access an employment opportunity. The Job Accommodation Network provides free, confidential guidance and examples.

A stronger decision rule

Include people in decisions that affect them.

Compassion is useful when it changes behavior: listen to lived experience, preserve choice, remove barriers, and make room for self-advocacy. Awareness without participation can still leave the same barriers in place.

Good default: ask what support is wanted, offer clear options, and let the person direct the next step whenever possible.
